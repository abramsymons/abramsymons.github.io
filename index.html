Ordering Before Execution

Eight years in decentralized systems, one idea, told in the order it happened.

For eight years in decentralized systems, my work has come down to one question: how do many machines that don't trust each other end up with the same state? My answer has been the same since 2018. Agree on the order of events first. Let each machine execute them itself. Then make that agreement as cheap and as safe to verify as possible.

A resume lists jobs. This page explains why each one led to the next.

entry 02018 – 2023

BrightID and the first replicated log

Core team developer, BrightID

BrightID lets people prove they use only one account, through a social graph of real connections. It grew past 73,000 verified users, and Gitcoin Grants was among the 15 apps that used it. I was the primary architect and developer of the node that stores the graph, with more than 600 commits.

The core problem was keeping many independent nodes in agreement about the graph. Putting every new connection on Ethereum mainnet would have cost users too much gas. Sidechains and L2s didn't exist yet. So we launched IDChain, a dedicated proof-of-authority chain, and I designed the nodes to read graph operations from it and replay them in block order. Every node ended up with the same graph without trusting the others. I didn't know it yet, but that design was the start of everything I've built since.

I also led the anti-sybil research. We tried SybilRank variants, built a group-based ranking algorithm and a simulator for attacking the graph, and I implemented Bitu, a clustering-based verification about 12,000 users had reached by 2022. When our CEO wanted stronger privacy, I designed and built blind-signature verifications, so an app can confirm someone is verified without anyone linking their accounts across apps.

What stayed with me: if every replica sees the same operations in the same order, you can keep consensus and execution apart.

entry 12021 – 2023

Muon: one signature from hundreds of nodes

Architect, then technical lead, Muon Network

Muon began as a decentralized price oracle for DEUS and Symmio. I designed its architecture in 2021, and from the first day I chose threshold signatures. With multisig, the cost of verifying on-chain grows with every signer. With a threshold Schnorr signature, hundreds of nodes produce one signature that costs the same to verify no matter how many signed. MPC was still rare in crypto then.

In 2022 I joined the team directly to help with security. The JavaScript node had grown large and complicated, and I concluded that rewriting a lean node in Python was safer than trying to audit the old one. I led about six engineers through it and taught them the FROST protocol. Along the way I filed fourteen security and design issues, including a rogue-key attack on the key generation and a way for a single node to block it. The network reached more than 3,400 testnet operators and a mainnet of over 1,500 nodes.

Some problems I cared about couldn't be solved inside an oracle, so I left to build Zellular.

What stayed with me: make the trusted part small enough to verify. A system you can't audit can't be trusted.

entry 22023 – 2025

Zellular: consensus as a service

Founder and protocol architect, Zellular

Zellular turned the BrightID lesson into a product. A dApp shouldn't need its own blockchain. It should be able to send transactions to a decentralized sequencer, read back a finalized order with a compact proof, and run its own logic in any language. I designed the protocol: a leader-based BFT design without proposer rotation or gossip, BLS-aggregated finality proofs, and a dispute path for replacing a faulty leader. We built it for 100,000 transactions per second through batching, with sub-second finality, and ran it as an EigenLayer AVS with about ten testnet operators.

To make EigenLayer usable outside Go, we wrote SDKs in Python and JavaScript with reference services. That won 1st place at EigenLayer's AVS MicroHacks in 2024, against more than 100 builders. The Eigen Foundation later gave Zellular a 100,000 EIGEN ecosystem grant.

The second service was custody. I wrote the pyfrost protocol specification, which works through secret sharing, distributed key generation and the rogue-key attack. It also shows how naive complaint handling lets two colluding nodes push honest ones out, which is why pyfrost uses an identifiable-cheating DKG. From there I designed zbtc, a Bitcoin bridge secured by restaked operators, and EigenSync, which makes those operators' signatures verifiable on any EVM chain. Both stayed prototypes, but they became a general multi-chain custody service.

What stayed with me: the hard part of a decentralized app is ordering and custody. Offer both as services and developers can focus on their logic.

entry 32024 – now

Zex: the demo that became a company

Founder and protocol architect, then tech lead and reviewer, Zex

Zex started as a demo: an exchange running as a replicated state machine on Zellular. It grew faster than Zellular itself, mostly because that's where the money in DeFi was, and in 2025 Zellular merged into it. I handed the business to a dedicated team, appointed a CTO, and stayed on as tech lead and reviewer, which I still am.

The custody service became ZexPorta. One validator group controls funds on every chain. On Bitcoin, each user gets their own Taproot address by tweaking the group key, and a withdrawal looks like an ordinary single-signature spend. On EVM and Tron, deposits land in CREATE2 contracts and a Schnorr verifier checks the threshold signature with the ecrecover trick. Solana and Aptos verify Ed25519 natively. An independent shield key must co-sign every withdrawal, so even a compromised threshold group can't move funds. For production, we replaced pyfrost's own cryptography with the Zcash Foundation's audited FROST and kept our Python networking around it.

I also designed wallet-free login. A user's session key is bound into a Google-signed token through the login challenge, and every replica checks it, so there's no custodial login provider. It turned out to be the same approach Aptos and Sui use for their keyless accounts. I also adapted passkeys, which assume a server, to apps that have none.

What stayed with me: build your own version to learn, then stand on audited cores before you hold anyone's money.

entry 4ongoing

Breaking other people's consensus

Independent security research

Designing consensus taught me where it breaks, so I started looking for those breaks in other chains, with AI-assisted review as part of my workflow. On Aptos I found a High-severity consensus bug that could halt the network, which earned a $30,000 bounty, and three more valid findings that turned out to be duplicates. On Sui I've reported eight issues. Six denial-of-service findings were confirmed as duplicates, four of them reachable by unauthenticated peers, and two are still under review.

What stayed with me: a duplicate still means you were right. The next step is to be first.

entry 52026

From prototype to production cores

Zex

Zellular proved the idea but was never meant for production. Now I'm doing for consensus what we did for custody. vseq takes Firedancer's implementation of Alpenglow, Solana's new consensus, and runs it as a standalone ordering service with no Solana execution. Apps verify BLS finality proofs themselves, exactly as they did with Zellular.

The matching engine got the same treatment. I redesigned the exchange protocol so two independent implementations can agree exactly: a strict grammar for the human-readable signed messages, 128-bit bounds on every quantity, a canonical state hash and conformance vectors. Then we prototyped a C engine and checked it against the Python one batch by batch. Zex was designed for 100,000 orders per second. The C prototype reaches about a million, around 830,000 on a realistic deep order book. When the first results looked too good, I didn't trust them until a standalone benchmark proved them.

I design these systems and direct AI-assisted implementations in languages I don't write daily, such as C. My job is the protocol, its safety properties, and reviewing what gets built.

What I keep relearning

  1. Never trust a single path. Two independent computations, a threshold of signers, or a co-signing key.
  2. Ordering is the shared problem. Execution can stay local.
  3. Make the trusted part small enough to verify.
  4. Prototype your own design to understand it, then ship on audited, production-tested cores.
  5. Doubt your own numbers until something independent confirms them.
